01 · THE BIG IDEA

Why reproduce?

Textbook pp. 2–7 · Learning notes

Reproduction produces new individuals and ensures the continuity of a living thing's kind. Without new offspring, a population may eventually die out as its members die.

Cells are the basic building blocks of living things. Different cells have different jobs. Reproductive cells carry information that is passed from parents to offspring. This helps explain why offspring have characteristics similar to their parents, though they are not necessarily identical.

Explore · Read the explanations

A magnified view of cells

A living thing has body parts made of cells. A microscope helps us see these tiny building blocks.
Think about it

Similar does not mean identical

A young plant may share features with its parent. Human offspring may also share features with their parents.

Hair length, a scar or a practised skill can change during a person's life. Do not assume every visible difference is inherited.

Reproduction helps a population continue. Its survival also depends on food, habitat and other conditions.

03 · A NEW LIFE

From fertilisation to birth

Textbook pp. 10–11 · Learning notes

Fertilisation is the fusion of a male reproductive cell with a female reproductive cell. In humans, one sperm fuses with one egg. The fertilised egg develops into an embryo and then a foetus in the womb. Development before birth usually takes about 40 weeks.

05 · MOVING POLLEN

Pollination

Textbook pp. 15–16 · Learning notes

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from an anther to a stigma. It can occur within one flower, between flowers on the same plant, or between flowers on different plants of the same kind. Animals and wind can carry pollen.

06 · AFTER POLLINATION

Flowers, fruits and seeds

Textbook p. 17 · Learning notes

A pollen grain on the stigma grows a pollen tube down the style towards an ovule. A male reproductive cell travels through the tube and fuses with the egg cell in the ovule. This fusion is fertilisation.

Try it · From flowers to a harvest

How many fruits? How many seeds?

One plant has five flowers. Each ovary holds six ovules. Decide which flowers are pollinated and how many ovules are fertilised in each, then count the harvest.

One flower has one ovary, so it can become at most one fruit. Each fertilised ovule can become one seed. A flower that is not pollinated, or whose ovules are not fertilised, usually withers and does not form a fruit.
Try it · Fruit or not?

Is it really a fruit?

A fruit develops from the ovary of a flower and usually contains seeds. Some foods we call vegetables are fruits, and some are not fruits at all.

07 · TRAVELLING SEEDS

Seed dispersal

Textbook pp. 18–19 · Learning notes

Dispersal spreads fruits or seeds away from the parent plant. This reduces overcrowding and competition for light, water, mineral salts and space. Look for a structure that helps a seed travel.

08 · A SEED STARTS GROWING

Seed germination

Textbook pp. 20–21 · Learning notes

A seed needs sufficient water, oxygen and a suitable temperature to germinate. Light and soil are not requirements for germination. A root emerges before the leaves. Stored nutrients in seed leaves support early growth; when true leaves appear, the young plant can begin making food.

    09 · ANOTHER WAY

    Reproduction by spores

    Textbook p. 21 · Learning notes

    Ferns and mosses reproduce by spores. Spores are different from seeds. In a fern, spore bags occur on the underside of fronds. Mosses produce spores in capsules. When mature spore bags or capsules open, spores are released and can be carried by wind or water. In favourable conditions they can develop into new plants.

    Try it · A fern's spore journey
    Be precise

    Which plants?

    Ferns and mosses are useful examples of plants that reproduce by spores.

    Do not turn this into “all non-flowering plants reproduce by spores”. Some other non-flowering plants, such as conifers, produce seeds.

    The fern's complete life cycle has additional stages. This model focuses on spore formation, release and growth, as in the chapter.

    Fungi such as mushrooms and bread mould also reproduce by spores, but fungi are not plants.

    10 · CONNECT THE IDEAS

    Plants and humans

    Textbook p. 22 · Learning notes

    Human and flowering-plant sexual reproduction both involve the fusion of a male reproductive cell with a female reproductive cell. Their structures and the ways the cells meet are different.

    Idea Humans Flowering plants
    Male reproductive cells Sperm produced by testes Contained in pollen grains in anthers
    Female reproductive cells Eggs produced by ovaries Egg cells inside ovules in the ovary
    Fusion of cells Fertilisation Fertilisation
    Special plant process No pollination Pollination transfers pollen to the stigma
    Development Embryo and foetus develop in womb Fertilised ovules develop into seeds
