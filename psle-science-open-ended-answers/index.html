How to answer PSLE Science open-ended questions.

Open-ended questions are where most children lose Science marks, often even when they understand the topic. This guide shows the Claim, Evidence, Reasoning method we teach at Polymath, with worked examples and the mistakes to avoid.

Step 1: read the question before you answer it.

Many wrong answers are good answers to a different question. Before writing, underline three things:

  1. What was changed. In an experiment this is the one thing the student varied, such as the material of a spoon or the amount of light.
  2. What was measured or observed. A temperature, a time, a height, a number of bubbles.
  3. The command word. “State” needs a short answer. “Explain” needs a reason. “Compare” needs both things mentioned. “Based on the graph” means the answer must use the graph.

If the question is about a fair test, also note what was kept the same, because a common question asks why.

Step 2: Claim, Evidence, Reasoning.

A full-mark explanation usually has three parts. At Polymath we teach them as CER:

  • Claim: a direct answer to the question, in one sentence.
  • Evidence: the data or observation from the question that supports the claim. Quote the numbers.
  • Reasoning: the science concept that links the evidence to the claim, using the correct keywords.

Not every question needs all three written out. A “state” question may need only the claim. But for “explain” questions, checking that the answer has a claim, evidence and reasoning catches most missing marks.

Two worked examples.

Example 1: heat conduction

A metal spoon and a wooden spoon of the same size are placed in hot water for the same time. Both handles start at 28°C. After heating, the metal handle is 44°C and the wooden handle is 30°C. Which material conducts heat better? Explain.

A weak answer: “The metal spoon is hotter.”

True, but it does not answer which material conducts heat better, uses no data and gives no reason.

Claim: Metal is a better conductor of heat than wood.

Evidence: In the same time, the temperature of the metal handle rose by 16°C, while the wooden handle rose by only 2°C.

Reasoning: Heat was conducted from the hot water along the metal spoon to its handle faster than through the wooden spoon.

Example 2: condensation

Water droplets appear on the outside of a glass of iced water. Explain how the droplets formed.

A weak answer: “The water leaked out of the glass.” or “The ice melted on the outside.”

Both describe the wrong source of the water.

Answer: The warmer water vapour in the surrounding air came into contact with the cooler outer surface of the glass. The water vapour lost heat to the glass and condensed into water droplets.

Notice the keywords: water vapour, warmer and cooler, lost heat, condensed. Each one carries marks.

Six mistakes that cost marks.

  1. Not using the data. If the question gives a table or graph, the answer should quote it.
  2. Everyday words instead of scientific ones. “Water vapour”, not “steam” or “air”. “Gains heat”, not “gets hot”.
  3. Mixing up heat and temperature. Heat is energy that flows; temperature is how hot something is.
  4. Half a comparison. “Metal is a good conductor” is weaker than “metal is a better conductor of heat than wood”.
  5. Describing instead of explaining. What happened is the evidence; why it happened is the reasoning. Explain questions need both.
  6. Answering a different variable. Check the answer is about the thing that was changed in the experiment, not something kept the same.

Questions parents often ask.

What does CER stand for in PSLE Science?

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning. The claim answers the question, the evidence comes from the data in the question, and the reasoning uses a science concept to link them.

Why does my child lose marks even when they understand the topic?

Usually because the answer is incomplete rather than wrong: it misses the data from the question, leaves out a keyword, or describes what happened without explaining why.

How can my child practise open-ended questions at home?

Practise with past-year questions, then check each answer for a claim, evidence and reasoning. Polymath students also use Polymath Online, which marks open-ended answers with AI feedback.
